How Ultracapacitors (EDLC) Replace Batteries in Sag Protection

Why a charge-storage mechanism with no chemical reaction is uniquely suited to short, powerful voltage sag events

2026-10-16

An EDLC (Electric Double Layer Capacitor, or ultracapacitor) stores energy by physically adsorbing ions from an electrolyte onto an electrode surface, rather than through a chemical reaction inside the electrode material as a battery does. Because there is no chemical reaction to drive, an EDLC can charge and discharge on a millisecond timescale and respond immediately to a short, high-power demand such as a voltage sag. WESCO’s TSP® uses an EDLC as its energy storage element, giving it a battery-free architecture that compensates for voltage sags without a conventional battery.

On a semiconductor line, a few hundred milliseconds of voltage drop and recovery is often enough to stop equipment before an operator even notices. Response to such an event cannot be judged from the energy store alone; the UPS topology and operating mode also matter. EDLCs provide a different storage approach for repeated high-power charge-discharge operation.

How an EDLC stores energy

An EDLC’s storage mechanism is the electric double layer. Ions physically arrange themselves at the interface between the electrode and the electrolyte during charging, and separate again during discharge, releasing the stored charge. This is a physical process of ion movement and arrangement, not an electrochemical oxidation-reduction reaction that alters the electrode material. As a result, EDLCs respond to charge and discharge demands very quickly and can deliver large currents over short intervals.

Distinguish energy storage from UPS response

A battery stores energy through an electrochemical reaction in which ions insert into and extract from the electrode material. Battery charge-discharge characteristics should be distinguished from UPS supply-transfer response, which depends on the UPS topology and operating mode. Repeated charge-discharge cycling also gradually degrades the electrode material, reducing usable capacity over time. Battery output, runtime, and suitability for repeated charge-discharge operation depend on the battery type and system design.

Why EDLCs fit short, high-power sags

Most voltage sags last from a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds, but during that brief window the load still needs its full rated power instantly. Per SEMI F47, semiconductor manufacturing equipment must continue operating through voltage drops to 80% for 1 second, 70% for 0.5 seconds, and 50% for 0.2 seconds. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve is another widely referenced standard describing the voltage-versus-time tolerance envelope equipment is expected to withstand. In this “short but deep” voltage-time region, an EDLC that can deliver high power instantly, without a chemical reaction, is structurally better matched to the event. Using a battery to absorb the same repeated sags turns each event into a partial charge-discharge cycle, accelerating battery wear over time.

A storage medium that doesn’t wear out from cycling

EDLC storage is suited to repeated charge-discharge operation, and TSP has no battery replacement task because it uses no batteries. EDLCs still need inspection and eventual replacement. The referenced three-phase manual gives an approximate 10-year energy-storage replacement interval; actual life and inspection intervals depend on the model, operating state, and environment.

How WESCO TSP applies this in practice

WESCO TSP is a voltage sag protector designed for low-voltage (LV, up to 1kV) applications. Under normal conditions, it stays in bypass, passing grid power through SCRs. When a sag is detected, the SCRs disconnect from the grid and switch to the EDLC discharge path. Transfer time and compensation duration must be checked against the selected model, protected load, operating conditions, and product specification. Built on this architecture, WESCO has supplied more than 50,000 units to over 900 companies across more than 15 countries over 25 years, serving industries where power quality is critical: semiconductor, display, automotive, battery manufacturing, and data centers.

Key takeaways

  • EDLCs store and release energy by physically moving and arranging ions, enabling millisecond-scale response.
  • UPS response depends on topology and operating mode as well as energy storage; battery life must be managed for its operating conditions.
  • Voltage sags are short, high-power events, and that profile structurally favors EDLC storage over battery storage.
  • TSP uses no batteries, but inspection and replacement of components, including EDLCs, must follow the model manual and operating conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the main difference between EDLCs and conventional batteries?
An EDLC stores energy by physically adsorbing ions onto an electrode surface, while a battery stores energy through a chemical reaction inside the electrode material. EDLCs are suited to short, repeated high-power charge-discharge cycles, but life and degradation still depend on conditions such as voltage and temperature.
Q. How often does an EDLC-based TSP need to be replaced?
TSP has no batteries, but its EDLCs still need inspection and eventual replacement. The referenced three-phase manual gives an approximate 10-year energy-storage replacement interval; actual replacement timing depends on the model, operating state, and environment.
Q. How long can an EDLC compensate for a voltage sag?
Transfer time describes the change of supply path; compensation duration describes how long stored energy supports the load. Transfer time and compensation duration must be checked against the selected model, protected load, operating conditions, and product specification.
