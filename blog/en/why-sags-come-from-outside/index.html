Why Well-Managed Plants Still Trip: Voltage Sags Come from Outside the Fence

Lightning, grid faults and nearby large-load starts: the external causes internal maintenance cannot reach

A voltage sag is a brief drop in supply voltage, and most of the sags that stop production lines originate outside the plant fence: lightning strikes, faults on the utility grid, and large loads starting up nearby. Because the disturbance arrives through the incoming supply, even a facility with excellent maintenance cannot prevent it from the inside. The practical defense is to assume sags will happen and protect the load side with voltage sag compensation.

Picture a plant with a spotless maintenance record. No alarms, no overdue inspections, no known faults. Then one afternoon a line stops, and the restart takes hours. The switchgear shows no fault. The power never went out; the voltage dipped for a moment and came back. Searching inside the plant for the cause leads nowhere.

Why a dip stops a line without a blackout

A sag is not an outage. The lights stay on, so the event is easy to miss. But drives, PLCs, relays and contactors can trip or misbehave on a short dip alone, and one tripped device can take down a whole process.

In continuous processes such as semiconductor and display manufacturing, a momentary dip can turn into a long recovery. The equipment is undamaged, but the process still has to be brought back.

Three external sources

  1. Lightning. A strike on a transmission or distribution line causes a short-circuit fault. Until protection clears it, voltage stays depressed across the connected network. The strike can be far from your plant; being on the same network is enough.
  2. Grid faults. Line-to-line and line-to-ground faults, or equipment failures on the utility system, pull voltage down until the fault is cleared. Reclosing attempts can repeat the dip.
  3. Nearby large-load starts. A large motor or process load on the same distribution system draws heavy starting current, and voltage at the shared connection point sags. A neighbor’s start-up can stop your line.

In all three cases the origin is beyond your metering point, in territory you do not control.

Why internal management cannot close the gap

Good upkeep lowers the number of faults inside the plant. It does nothing about the quality of the voltage being delivered to the plant. The published references make the scale of the problem clear:

  • Frequency. IEEE studies report roughly 60-70 voltage sags per year at a typical industrial site. These are routine events, not rare accidents.
  • Equipment tolerance. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve is a published envelope of how much voltage deviation, for how long, equipment can generally tolerate.
  • Semiconductor ride-through. Per SEMI F47, tools should ride through 80% of nominal voltage for 1 s, 70% for 0.5 s, and 50% for 0.2 s.

A production line mixes many devices with different tolerances. The weakest one sets the line’s real ride-through. Even when the major tools meet the standard, a sag deeper or longer than the standard covers will still trip something.

How to prepare

Since the sources cannot be removed, plan around them:

  1. Record. Log voltage at the incoming supply with a power quality analyzer and match timestamps against your stoppage history. Confirm that sags are the cause before spending money.
  2. Rank the vulnerable loads. Identify the devices that trip most often and the lines where a stop costs the most. Compare them with the ITIC curve and SEMI F47 points.
  3. Review settings. Check undervoltage detection thresholds and restart conditions for unnecessary sensitivity.
  4. Compensate the critical loads. Install a voltage sag compensator upstream of the loads that matter most, so voltage is held through the dip.
