Voltage Sag vs Momentary Outage Difference: How to Tell Them Apart in the Field

A voltage sag keeps power on while dropping in magnitude, and an outage removes it. Here is how to separate the two.

A voltage sag and an outage differ in depth and duration: a voltage sag reduces voltage to 10-90% of nominal for 0.5 cycle to 1 minute and then recovers, so power stays connected, while an outage drops voltage to 0% for seconds to hours and interrupts supply. Korean industry commonly calls a voltage sag a “momentary outage” (순간정전), so the true contrast is between a sag and an outage. Both can originate from the same fault on the distribution network, yet they affect equipment in very different ways. Plant staff often see a process stop with no outage record and assume a supply failure that never occurred.

Consider a fault on a feeder shared by two customers. The customer nearest the fault loses power outright. A neighbor on the same network sees only a brief dip, and its drives or control relays trip anyway. The neighbor’s logs show no outage, so the stoppage looks unexplained. That pattern is the signature of a voltage sag.

Defining the two phenomena

  • Voltage sag: a reduction of voltage to 10-90% of nominal, lasting from 0.5 cycle to 1 minute, after which voltage recovers. Power remains connected. Commonly called a momentary outage in the industry.
  • Outage: a loss of voltage to 0%, interrupting supply for seconds to hours until service is restored.

The deciding factor is how far voltage falls. A partial reduction is a sag. A drop to zero is an outage.

Short voltage dips occur far more often than outages, so a stoppage with no outage record should be checked for a sag first.

Why neighbors stop without an outage

Sags spread across a network in a way outages do not. A fault close to one customer produces an outage there, while customers further along the same feeder see only a sag. Those customers have no outage to point to, which makes the root cause hard to trace.

The practical lesson is to read the event data before replacing equipment. A stoppage with no outage record usually points to a sag that tripped sensitive loads.

Field checkpoints for telling them apart

  1. Check outage records: Confirm with the utility or meter data whether an interruption occurred at that time. No record suggests a sag.
  2. Log the voltage: Use a power quality analyzer to capture the magnitude and duration of the dip. A drop to 10 to 90 percent of nominal that recovers is a sag.
  3. Compare the stopped equipment: If several loads stopped at the same moment, the cause is likely on the supply side. If only one stopped, check its own control settings first.
  4. Compare against a tolerance curve: Per SEMI F47, semiconductor tools should ride through specified dips, including 80 percent for 1 second, 70 percent for 0.5 seconds and 50 percent for 0.2 seconds. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve gives a published envelope for equipment tolerance to voltage variation.

Key takeaways

  • A voltage sag (commonly called a momentary outage in Korea) keeps power connected while voltage drops to 10-90% of nominal; an outage takes voltage to zero.
  • The same network fault can cause an outage at the fault site and only a sag at nearby customers.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the difference between a voltage sag (momentary outage) and an outage?
A voltage sag is a reduction of voltage to 10-90% of nominal lasting 0.5 cycle to 1 minute, and power remains connected. Korean industry commonly calls it a momentary outage (순간정전). An outage is a loss of voltage to 0% lasting seconds to hours, so the supply is interrupted.
Q. Why does equipment stop during a voltage sag if power is not lost?
When voltage drops below the operating range of control circuits, drives, or power supplies, protective functions can trip the equipment even though the supply never fully disconnects.
Q. How can a facility protect against voltage sags?
Utilities and designers reduce sag sources at the system level, and facilities can add compensation at the load side to ride through short dips. For low-voltage equipment, an EDLC-based voltage sag compensator such as WESCO TSP® can be evaluated.
