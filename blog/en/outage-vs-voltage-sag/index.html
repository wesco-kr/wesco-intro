Voltage Sag vs Momentary Outage Difference: How to Tell Them Apart in the Field

A voltage sag keeps power on while dropping in magnitude, and an outage removes it. Here is how to separate the two.

Voltage sag vs momentary outage difference is easiest to understand by asking whether voltage fell or the supply was interrupted. A sag is a temporary reduction in voltage. In Korean industrial conversation, “순간정전” may also refer to a sag, so the wording alone cannot identify the event. Check a voltage recording.

“The lights stayed on, but the machine stopped.” That can happen when a controller, drive or contactor leaves its operating range. It does not prove a supply-side sag: internal faults and protection trips can produce a similar symptom.

WESCO’s definition describes a sag as voltage falling to 10–90% of nominal for 0.5 cycle to 1 minute. This percentage is the voltage remaining, not the percentage lost. An interruption can also be brief; duration alone does not distinguish the two.

Why equipment may stay stopped after voltage returns

Consider an illustrative example, not a customer case: a pump contactor drops out during a dip in its control supply, releasing a seal-in circuit. If the circuit requires a new start command or an alarm reset, the pump can remain stopped after voltage returns.

A circuit with a maintained run command may re-energize instead. Recovery depends on the control circuit, interlocks and restart settings, not simply on whether the contactor has a mechanical latch. Do not bypass safety interlocks to force an automatic restart.

Checks that narrow down the cause

  1. Align the timestamps of the equipment stoppage, power-quality recording and controller alarms.
  2. Read voltage magnitude and duration. Absence of a utility outage notice does not establish that a sag occurred.
  3. Identify whether several loads stopped together or only one did. Treat that pattern as a clue, not a diagnosis.
  4. Compare the main supply, control supply, coil command and drive alarms to establish the sequence.
  5. Check the actual model’s permitted voltage, coil type, temperature conditions and restart logic.

Key takeaways

  • Separate reduced voltage from an interrupted supply using recorded data.
  • Investigate internal faults as well as sags when no outage is recorded.
  • Recovery depends on the control circuit and protection logic.
  • Select TSP protection around the actual load and circuit boundary.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the difference between a voltage sag (momentary outage) and an outage?
A sag reduces voltage while an interruption removes the supply. Korean field usage of 순간정전 may include sags; use recorded voltage magnitude and duration to distinguish the events.
Q. Why does equipment stop during a voltage sag if power is not lost?
When voltage drops below the operating range of control circuits, drives, or power supplies, protective functions can trip the equipment even though the supply never fully disconnects.
Q. How can a facility protect against voltage sags?
Utilities and designers reduce sag sources at the system level, and facilities can add compensation at the load side to ride through short dips. For low-voltage equipment, an EDLC-based voltage sag compensator such as WESCO TSP® can be evaluated.
