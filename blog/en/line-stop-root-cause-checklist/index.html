Equipment stop investigation — records to check before assigning a cause

A review note for voltage records, control commands and contactor specifications

2026-11-10

An equipment stop investigation begins with a question: do you have the records needed to assess the stop? An absent outage record does not prove a voltage sag. This checklist asks for voltage, control-command and alarm records, together with the actual contactor specification and restart circuit.

Equipment stop investigation: check the available records

Ask the person reviewing the stop for the following information. These are requests for evidence, not a formula for assigning a cause.

  • Is there a voltage record for the supply to the affected equipment at the time of the stop?
  • Can coil or control-command records and equipment alarms be compared on a common time basis?
  • Have the available records been separated from information that is still missing?

If no voltage record is available, do not record a voltage sag as confirmed. Whether other machines stopped can be noted, but do not use that observation alone to establish an internal fault or a supply-system cause.

Read a contactor specification with its conditions

Contactor dropout is the release of the contacts when the coil loses its energized state. Schneider Electric’s TeSys D guidance separates dropout-voltage ranges by model group, AC/DC control circuit and temperature. A single percentage therefore cannot serve as the stop threshold for every contactor.

In the review note, identify the model, AC or DC coil, applicable temperature and the specification being consulted. Leave missing values unresolved instead of estimating them.

Reference: Schneider Electric’s TeSys D dropout conditions. This article explains the distinctions to check; it does not reproduce the manufacturer’s tables or diagrams.

Check the restart circuit after supply recovery

Supply recovery and equipment restart are separate questions. In the three-wire holding arrangement described by Schneider Electric, a supply or control interruption releases the held state and a fresh start signal is needed. In a two-wire arrangement, a maintained command can allow restart when supply returns.

That explanation describes those control arrangements; it does not guarantee the response of every machine. Check the actual wiring, operating command, interlocks and equipment-specific restart requirements. Do not bypass safety interlocks to obtain a restart.

Reference: Schneider Electric’s explanation of two-wire and three-wire control.

Define the circuit being protected

When considering WESCO TSP for control-power protection, identify the control circuit that would be connected. Protecting control power does not establish that an unprotected motor or drive power circuit, or the complete machine, will continue operating. Check the selected model’s specifications, wiring and operating conditions separately.

Key takeaways

  • Collect voltage, control-command and alarm records; keep missing evidence marked as unconfirmed.
  • Read contactor specifications with model, AC/DC and temperature conditions.
  • Check the actual control circuit and interlocks for restart behavior.
  • Distinguish control-power protection from whole-machine operation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Does the absence of an outage record prove a voltage sag?
No. Check voltage, control-command and equipment alarm records together with the actual product specification.
Q. Do all contactors have the same dropout voltage?
No. Schneider Electric’s TeSys D guidance distinguishes dropout-voltage ranges by model group, AC/DC control circuit and temperature. A value for one product is not a universal threshold.
Q. Will equipment restart as soon as voltage returns?
It depends on the circuit. The manufacturer's three-wire holding arrangement requires a fresh start signal after interruption; a maintained two-wire command can permit restart on supply recovery. Check the actual interlocks and restart requirements.
