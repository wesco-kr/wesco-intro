Momentary Outage Damage in Factories: Cases and Equipment Trip Thresholds

Why inverters, servo drives, and PLCs stop first, and how losses extend past the trip

Momentary outage damage is the production loss that occurs when a voltage sag or brief interruption trips factory equipment. A voltage sag is a short drop in supply voltage, while a momentary interruption is the deeper case where voltage nearly disappears. Both often come from the same grid events, and equipment responds differently depending on depth and duration. In most plants, the damage does not end when the line stops. Scrap, restarts, quality losses, and delivery delays follow.

Consider a line running at 2 p.m. when a fault on a nearby feeder pulls supply voltage down for a moment. The dip is brief. An inverter trips first, the PLC resets soon after, and by the time operators begin recovery, work in progress has already been scrapped. This scenario is illustrative, but the pattern is common.

Voltage sags are more frequent than most plants expect

IEEE studies report roughly 60 to 70 voltage sags per year at a typical industrial site. Most originate outside the plant, from faults on neighboring feeders or from the starting of large loads on the same network. Many sags are too short to notice, yet sensitive equipment reacts to them anyway.

Equipment does not respond to the same sag in the same way

Ride-through capability depends on power electronics, control circuits, and protection settings. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve is widely used as a published tolerance envelope. Operation inside the curve is generally expected, while events outside it are likely to cause trips. Semiconductor tools are typically specified against SEMI F47, which defines voltage sag ride-through points. Exact limits vary by machine, so the manufacturer’s data sheet should be the reference for each installation.

Trip order: drives first, then controls

Under the same sag, trips usually follow a recognizable sequence:

  1. Inverters and servo drives: undervoltage protection on the DC bus typically trips first.
  2. PLCs and control power supplies: unstable control voltage can reset I/O and restart programs.
  3. Contactors and relays: they drop out when coil voltage falls below its holding level.
  4. Motors and fans: they coast for a while, then decelerate and stop.

This order helps set priorities in a site audit. Check drive undervoltage settings first, then confirm the margin of the control power supply.

How momentary outage damage grows by industry

  • Semiconductor: an interrupted process step can scrap wafers or lots, and tool recovery and process stabilization add further time.
  • Paper: a high-speed web can break during a sudden stop, requiring roll changes and rethreading.
  • Printing: each stoppage wastes stock, and registration and ink density must be reset before good output resumes.
  • Chemical: interrupted reactions may need purging and restart, with contamination risk in the lines.

In each case, the stop itself is the smallest cost. Restarts, quality checks, and missed delivery windows usually add more.

Key takeaways

  • Momentary outage damage starts with the trip but grows through scrap, restarts, quality loss, and delays.
  • The same voltage sag affects equipment differently. Inverters and servo drives usually trip first, followed by PLCs and control power.
  • Industry impact varies. Semiconductor lots, paper webs, printed stock, and chemical processes each carry distinct costs.
