A voltage sag is a short-duration drop in supply voltage — down to 10-90% of nominal — lasting anywhere from half a cycle (about 8-10ms) to a full minute before the voltage recovers on its own. Unlike a blackout, power is never fully lost, so the event is often invisible to the naked eye, yet it’s enough to trip sensitive equipment on semiconductor, display, and automotive lines. In plant conversations, “voltage dip,” “voltage sag,” and “sag” all refer to the same event.

A lightning strike near a substation, or a large motor starting on the same distribution feeder, can cause voltage to waver for tens of milliseconds. No blackout alarm fires, yet the line stops anyway. Per IEEE studies, a typical industrial site experiences roughly 60-70 voltage sags per year, and many of these get logged as unexplained stoppages because no one traces them back to the grid event that caused them. The questions below cover what engineers ask most once they start investigating.

What causes a voltage sag?

Most sags trace back to grid-side faults or sudden load changes:

  • Line-to-ground or line-to-line faults on distribution feeders, caused by lightning, animal contact, or vegetation
  • Large nearby loads (motors, welders) drawing a surge of current at startup
  • Protective relay operations that isolate a faulted section of the grid
  • Sudden load swings at points of interconnection

Because these events resolve as soon as the faulted section is isolated, they’re brief by nature — nothing like the extended duration of a true outage.

How is a voltage sag different from an outage?

Both are short, which is why they get confused, but the mechanism is different. An outage is a near-total loss of voltage — power is simply gone. A voltage sag is a partial drop, say to 50% or 70% of nominal, that recovers within cycles. An outage is a “loss”; a sag is a “wobble.” That distinction doesn’t make sags harmless, though — PLCs, servo drives, and other precision controls can reset or alarm out from a brief dip alone, producing a stoppage that looks identical to what an outage would cause.

How vulnerable is our equipment, really?

Industry standards give a useful benchmark. Per SEMI F47, semiconductor process equipment is expected to ride through a sag to 80% of nominal for 1 second, 70% for 0.5 seconds, and 50% for 0.2 seconds. Across electronics more broadly, the ITIC (CBEMA) curve is the commonly cited reference — a published envelope showing the sag depth and duration that typical equipment can tolerate without disruption. These are design targets, not guarantees: older equipment and sensitive controllers in the field often trip well inside the boundaries the standards allow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What exactly is a voltage sag?
A voltage sag is a short-duration drop in voltage to 10-90% of nominal, lasting between 0.5 cycles and one minute before recovering. Unlike a blackout, power is never fully lost, but the brief dip alone can trip or reset sensitive equipment.
Q. Is a voltage sag the same thing as a momentary outage?
No. A voltage sag is a partial drop in voltage that recovers on its own, while an outage is a near-total loss of voltage. Both are short in duration, which is why the terms get used interchangeably on the plant floor.
