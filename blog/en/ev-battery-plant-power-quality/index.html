Power Quality in EV Battery Plants: Why Sags on Electrode and Assembly Lines Turn Into Batch Losses

How continuous coating and assembly processes raise sensitivity to voltage sags

2026-10-09
EV BatteryBattery ManufacturingPower QualityVoltage SagEDLC
한국어

EV battery plants run electrode coating, drying, and assembly as continuous lines, so a single voltage sag can throw off coating thickness, tension, or alignment across an entire coil or lot rather than stopping one machine. Servo motors on coating heads and winding or stacking equipment must hold precise speed and position even through momentary voltage dips, making them more sensitive to short disturbances than typical industrial equipment. As a result, power quality strategy for battery manufacturing lines needs to address brief voltage sags, not just full outages.

Electrode Process: Continuous Coating Raises Sensitivity

The electrode process runs as a roll-to-roll line: slurry coating, drying, calendaring, and slitting. Servo motors driving the coating head and rollers must maintain constant speed and web tension to keep coating thickness uniform. A voltage dip lasting only tens or hundreds of milliseconds can disturb servo torque enough to shift coating thickness — and that disturbance runs the length of the coil, not just a single point. Everything from the moment of the sag to the next inspection point can be flagged as defective, making the affected zone far larger than one machine stoppage.

Assembly Process: Precision Control in Winding and Stacking

Winding and stacking equipment layers electrodes and separators with micrometer-level alignment. Vision cameras and servo drives both respond to voltage fluctuations, so a voltage sag can translate directly into misalignment or cell-shape defects. Assembly lines run slower than electrode lines, but their tighter alignment tolerance often makes them even more sensitive to momentary voltage disturbances.

Why Losses Spread to the Batch Level

Battery manufacturing stages are tightly linked. A disturbance during electrode coating either gets scrapped before reaching assembly, or passes through and gets caught as a defect downstream. Either way, the loss extends beyond the instant of the sag to the entire coil or lot that contains it. It isn’t one machine going down — it’s an entire process segment becoming rework or scrap.

The Gap Between Equipment Ride-Through and Real Sags

Per SEMI F47, semiconductor and precision manufacturing equipment is expected to ride through 80% voltage for 1 second, 70% for 0.5 seconds, and 50% for 0.2 seconds. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve similarly defines a tolerance envelope for how industrial and electronic equipment should behave during brief voltage sags. In practice, however, servo motors and vision systems on battery lines often react to disturbances shorter than these published envelopes, so process output can still be affected even when equipment technically meets ride-through standards.

Where Standard UPS Falls Short

Battery-based UPS systems are built for full outages, but their transfer logic can be too slow — or may not engage at all — for shallow, short-duration sags. Lead-acid and lithium batteries also require periodic replacement and inspection, which becomes a maintenance risk on battery lines that run continuously around the clock.
