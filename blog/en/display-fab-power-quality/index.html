Power Quality on Display Panel Lines: Why 24/7 Processes Can't Tolerate Sags

How voltage sags turn into work-in-process losses on continuous OLED and TFT-LCD lines

2026-09-08

OLED and TFT-LCD panel lines run vacuum deposition, exposure, etching, and thermal treatment as a continuous process that can stretch tens of hours without a break. Because of this, a voltage sag lasting only a few milliseconds can trip equipment and force the fab to restart or scrap whatever work-in-process (WIP) was mid-cycle at that moment. That structural vulnerability is exactly why display lines need a compensation approach that doesn’t depend on batteries to ride through the disturbance.

Consider an OLED deposition chamber eight hours into a vacuum process when a nearby lightning strike or the starting current of a large piece of equipment causes a voltage sag lasting roughly 0.1 second. The chamber’s internal vacuum level and temperature profile can shift enough to scrap the entire batch. This scenario isn’t rare on display lines — a single trip on one tool ripples through upstream and downstream processes, delaying the whole production schedule.

Why display processes are so sensitive to voltage disturbances

  • Vacuum deposition (OLED), exposure, etching, and thermal treatment are continuous processes that cannot be paused once started.
  • Vacuum level, temperature, and pressure control inside process chambers respond to power disturbances measured in milliseconds.
  • A single tool trip cascades into connected upstream and downstream steps, delaying the entire production schedule.

Why voltage sags happen in the first place

A voltage sag is a short-duration drop in rated voltage, typically lasting a few cycles up to a few hundred milliseconds, most often caused by lightning strikes, grid faults, or the inrush current of large equipment starting up nearby. IEEE studies report roughly 60-70 voltage sags per year at a typical industrial site. For a display line running 24/7, that’s not a rare event — it’s a recurring exposure that calls for dedicated protection, distinct from how a facility might handle a full power outage.

How much ride-through is equipment expected to have?

The power quality industry has published reference standards for how long equipment should tolerate a sag before it’s expected to fail:

  • Per SEMI F47, equipment should ride through a sag to 80% of rated voltage for 1 second, to 70% for 0.5 seconds, and to 50% for 0.2 seconds.
  • The ITIC (CBEMA) curve is another widely referenced equipment-tolerance envelope: voltage excursions inside the curve should not disrupt normal equipment operation.

In practice, though, precision display and semiconductor tools are often far more sensitive than these reference standards, so meeting the standard alone isn’t enough — dedicated voltage sag compensation is still needed.

What happens after a trip: the WIP-loss structure

When equipment trips, power returning to normal doesn’t mean production resumes immediately. The line stays down through purge, vacuum re-establishment, and temperature re-stabilization steps, and whatever WIP was inside the chamber at the moment of the trip may fall outside spec and require rework or scrapping. A single voltage sag can therefore cause WIP loss across multiple process stages — a defining characteristic of display fab exposure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is a voltage sag, exactly?
A voltage sag is a short-duration drop in supply voltage, typically lasting a few cycles to a few hundred milliseconds. Unlike a full outage, voltage doesn't disappear entirely, but even that brief dip can cause sensitive equipment to malfunction or trip.
Q. Why are display fabs especially vulnerable to voltage sags?
OLED deposition and TFT-LCD exposure or thermal processes run continuously for tens of hours without interruption. If equipment trips mid-process, the fab faces both a restart procedure and the loss of whatever work-in-process was inside the chamber at that moment.
