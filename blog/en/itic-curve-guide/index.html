How to Read the ITIC Curve: Knowing the Voltage Envelope Your Equipment Tolerates

Reading the axes and regions of the ITIC (CBEMA) curve to decide when a compensator is needed

The ITIC curve, successor to the CBEMA curve, is a published standard graph that defines the voltage range information technology equipment must tolerate without malfunctioning. Its horizontal axis plots the duration of a voltage deviation (milliseconds to seconds) and its vertical axis plots voltage as a percentage of nominal; any sag or surge that falls inside the curve’s envelope is expected to leave equipment operating normally. Overlaying real measurement data on this curve is often enough to tell you, at a glance, whether a given event was severe enough to put your equipment at risk.

A common scenario looks like this: a production line stops for no obvious reason, there was no full power outage, and the voltage meter shows nothing dramatic. Plotting the recorded sag against the ITIC curve frequently reveals that a brief, shallow dip — one that seemed harmless — actually fell outside the equipment’s tolerance envelope. Voltage sags are easy to miss but remain one of the more common causes of unplanned equipment stops.

Three regions to read

The ITIC curve is built from three regions.

  • Prohibited region: above the curve, covering overvoltage and surge conditions. Events here risk equipment damage and must be suppressed.
  • Acceptable (tolerance) region: inside the curve. Equipment is expected to keep operating normally even when voltage dips or rises briefly — this is the region that matters most when deciding whether a compensator is needed.
  • No damage region: below the curve, covering deep sags and brief interruptions. Equipment may stop, but in principle should not be damaged — though in practice this often still means line stops and scrap.

The key pattern is that the shorter the duration, the deeper a sag the curve still classifies as tolerable. In other words, the curve’s underlying assumption is that equipment should be designed to ride through brief dips.

Checking whether your equipment fits inside the curve

  1. Check the equipment’s power specification sheet for its input voltage tolerance and ride-through time.
  2. Collect power quality measurement data on-site — sag magnitude and duration.
  3. Plot each recorded event on the ITIC curve to see whether it lands inside the acceptable region or crosses into the no-damage/damage region.
  4. If events repeatedly land in the damage region, evaluate adding a voltage sag compensator upstream of that equipment.

This is a diagnostic you can run with nothing more than power quality meter data and the curve itself — a natural first step before commissioning new equipment or investigating recurring stoppages.

Reading it alongside SEMI F47

For semiconductor and display fabs, the ITIC curve should be read together with SEMI F47. SEMI F47 sets ride-through requirements for semiconductor manufacturing equipment: equipment must ride through 1 second at 80% voltage, 0.5 seconds at 70%, and 0.2 seconds at 50% (per SEMI F47). Where the ITIC curve is a general benchmark for information technology equipment broadly, SEMI F47 is a stricter, process-specific reference point for fab equipment. Comparing your data against both makes it much clearer how much margin — or how little — your equipment actually has.

How often do out-of-envelope events really happen

IEEE studies report that a typical industrial site experiences roughly 60 to 70 voltage sags per year. Not every one of these causes a full outage, but if a meaningful share of them sit near or beyond the ITIC curve’s tolerance boundary, equipment is being repeatedly exposed to malfunction risk. In industries where a single unplanned stop is costly — semiconductor, display, automotive, battery, and data center facilities — checking this curve periodically is worth building into routine power quality reviews.

Key takeaways

  • The ITIC curve defines a voltage tolerance envelope for IT equipment using duration (x-axis) and voltage percentage (y-axis).
  • Reading it as three regions — prohibited, acceptable, no-damage — lets you classify where a real event falls.
  • Semiconductor and display equipment should be checked against both the ITIC curve and SEMI F47 ride-through points (80%/1s, 70%/0.5s, 50%/0.2s).

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Are the ITIC curve and the CBEMA curve the same thing?
The ITIC curve is the updated version of the CBEMA curve, published by the Information Technology Industry Council. Because they serve the same purpose, the two names are often used interchangeably in practice.
Q. Does an event outside the ITIC curve always mean equipment failure?
Not necessarily, but it does mean a higher risk of malfunction or unplanned stops. Actual tolerance varies by equipment, so the curve should be used as a reference benchmark rather than an absolute guarantee.
Q. How is SEMI F47 different from the ITIC curve?
The ITIC curve is a general-purpose benchmark for information technology equipment, while SEMI F47 is a ride-through requirement specific to semiconductor manufacturing equipment. Both address voltage sag tolerance, but they apply to different equipment and different reference points.
