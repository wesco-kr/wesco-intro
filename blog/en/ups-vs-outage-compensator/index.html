Momentary outage UPS difference: Battery Backup vs. Voltage Sag Compensation

Outages and voltage dips are different problems. A load-based guide to choosing the right device

Momentary outage UPS difference comes down to the event each device is built to handle. A UPS (uninterruptible power supply) carries a load through a supply interruption using stored energy, typically a battery. A voltage sag compensator bridges a short drop in voltage so sensitive equipment keeps running; TSP® also compensates a complete loss of voltage for 1 to 3 seconds. The two are not rivals: momentary outages and sags lasting a few seconds or less are handled by the compensator, and longer outages by the UPS. The right choice depends on the load.

Consider a PLC cabinet on a production line that drops out during a dip lasting less than a second. The utility log shows no outage, and the switchgear looks normal. Investigating such trips starts with separating outages from voltage sags.

Outages and voltage sags are different events

A sag is a short reduction to 10-90% of nominal that recovers within 0.5 cycle to 1 minute, while an outage is a complete loss of supply lasting seconds to hours. Korean industry commonly calls a sag a momentary outage. Both can stop equipment, but they call for different protection.

Equipment tolerance is described with published references. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve plots the magnitude and duration of voltage deviations that equipment can typically withstand. For semiconductor tools, SEMI F47 defines ride-through points: according to SEMI F47, equipment should tolerate 80% voltage for 1 second, 70% for 0.5 seconds, and 50% for 0.2 seconds. Check your equipment specification against these points before choosing a device.

Momentary outage UPS difference: energy source and runtime

Battery UPS systems store energy in lead-acid or lithium batteries. Batteries degrade over time, so they need periodic testing and replacement, and their life depends on operating temperature.

TSP® (The Second Power) is an EDLC-based voltage sag protector. It stores energy in EDLC ultracapacitors, and the energy storage section carries a 10-year design life, so routine battery replacement is not needed. The energy source shapes both the maintenance workload and how long the unit can support a load.

How each device behaves in everyday operation

Everyday operation depends on the UPS topology. An online double-conversion UPS feeds the load through its inverter at all times. A standby UPS connects utility power directly to the load and switches to battery only when supply fails.

TSP® normally sits in bypass, with utility power connected to the load through a conducting SCR while its controller monitors the voltage. When a sag is detected, the SCR disconnects the utility and EDLC discharge takes over. This switchover completes within 2 ms. The 2 ms figure is transfer time, not compensation time. The compensation period is 1 to 3 seconds for small and medium capacities and 3 seconds for large capacities. After the supply recovers, the unit returns to bypass standby.

Match the device to the load class

  1. Loads that must survive long outages, such as servers, network equipment, and emergency lighting. A battery UPS sized to the required runtime is the right tool.
  2. Loads that trip on short dips, such as PLCs, instrumentation, sensors, and variable-speed drives. Voltage sag compensation addresses this failure mode directly.
  3. Mixed facilities. Assign devices by load group rather than by building, because one site can contain both classes.

WESCO’s core industries are semiconductors, displays, automotive, secondary batteries, and PCB. Their control and process equipment makes load classification the natural first step.

Compare maintenance and lifecycle

A battery UPS requires planning for replacement cycles, spent-battery disposal, and maintenance windows for battery work. Each added task raises the operating workload over the system’s life.

An EDLC-based energy storage section avoids those cycles. Its 10-year design life means the maintenance schedule has no battery replacement step. Confirm inspection items and life conditions in the product specification before purchase.

Check scope before you specify

TSP® covers low-voltage systems at 1 kV and below. If the goal is outage backup, start from a UPS design. If the goal is preventing trips caused by voltage dips, evaluate a voltage sag compensator. Identifying the failure mode first prevents specifying a device that solves the wrong problem.

Key takeaways

  • The momentary outage UPS difference lies in duration: momentary outages and sags of a few seconds or less call for sag compensation, while longer outages call for a UPS.
  • A battery UPS supports loads for the runtime they need.
  • An EDLC-based voltage sag protector such as TSP® maintains supply for 1 to 3 seconds and keeps control equipment from tripping.
  • Check voltage class (1 kV and below), load class, and energy storage design life before specifying.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. What is the difference between a momentary outage and a voltage sag?
An outage is a complete loss of supply voltage lasting seconds to hours. A voltage sag is a short reduction to 10-90% of nominal that recovers on its own, and in Korean industry it is commonly called a momentary outage (순간정전). Both can stop sensitive equipment: a compensator covers momentary outages and sags lasting a few seconds or less, and a UPS covers longer outages.
Q. Can an EDLC-based voltage sag protector replace a UPS?
No. TSP® is an EDLC-based voltage sag protector, not a UPS. Emergency loads and loads that need backup during long outages still require a UPS. Where the failure mode is a voltage dip that trips equipment, an EDLC-based compensator is worth evaluating.
Q. What voltage range does TSP® cover?
TSP® is designed for low-voltage systems at 1 kV and below. Compensation lasts 1 to 3 seconds for small and medium capacities and 3 seconds for large capacities.
