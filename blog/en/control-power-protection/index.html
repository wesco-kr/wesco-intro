Protect the Control Power, Save the Line: The Selective Compensation Approach

A design method that compensates only control power and critical loads instead of an entire facility, cutting investment while preventing line stoppages

2026-09-23

Selective compensation is a design approach that applies a voltage sag protector only to the control power and critical loads that actually cause a line stoppage, rather than to an entire facility. Most production stoppages start not in the power section but in the control power feeding PLCs, relays, and sensors — when that circuit drops, the control logic resets and the line stops. Protecting only that point can deliver the same stoppage-prevention result at a fraction of the investment.

The pattern is a familiar one on the plant floor. A voltage sag occurs, and the motors or heaters — the power section — ride through it on inertia. But the relay controlling that equipment drops first, the interlock releases, and the whole line halts. The root cause is a control signal interruption, not a power loss. IEEE studies report roughly 60-70 voltage sags per year at a typical industrial site, and protecting the control power point alone can prevent a large share of the stoppages those events would otherwise cause.

Why stoppages start at control power, not the power section

Motors and heaters often ride through a sag for a short period thanks to rotational or thermal inertia. Control power has no such buffer: the moment voltage drops below a threshold, relays open or PLCs reset, and the control logic is interrupted instantly. In most cases, the actual trigger for a line stoppage is a lost control signal rather than lost power — which is exactly the point selective compensation targets first.

Designing selective compensation — choosing which loads to cover

Rather than listing every load in the facility, selective compensation starts from the loads directly tied to a stoppage:

  • Control power (PLCs, relays, sequencers, interlock circuits)
  • Critical sensors or instrumentation with long recovery times
  • Drive components where a restart itself causes process loss

Loads outside this list can often ride through a voltage sag without triggering a stoppage, so excluding them from the compensation scope is what keeps the investment down.

Why TSP’s battery-free design fits selective compensation

Setting the compensation threshold with SEMI F47 and the ITIC curve

Published standards are a practical reference for deciding how deep and how long a sag needs to be covered. SEMI F47 defines ride-through points of 80% voltage for 1 second, 70% for 0.5 seconds, and 50% for 0.2 seconds. The ITIC (CBEMA) curve is another published envelope describing the voltage and duration range equipment is expected to tolerate. Control power compensation should be sized to cover the region that falls below these reference lines.

A staged approach to controlling investment

  1. Review recent stoppage history to determine whether the cause is the power section or control power.
  2. Build a list of the control power and critical loads directly tied to those stoppages.
  3. Size the TSP compensation scope to that load list only.
  4. Expand to additional process sections in stages if needed.

Following this sequence instead of sizing for the whole facility at once keeps the stoppage-prevention benefit while controlling upfront investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q. Which facilities are a good fit for selective compensation?
Facilities where line stoppages originate from a control power interruption rather than the power section itself — PLCs, relays, and sensors dropping before the motors or heaters do. Confirming that sequence is the first step.
Q. How does selective compensation differ from whole-facility compensation?
Whole-facility compensation must cover the capacity of every load, while selective compensation only needs to cover the control power and critical loads that actually cause stoppages, which reduces the required TSP capacity and installation scope.
Q. Why is a battery-free design like TSP well suited to selective compensation?
TSP uses an EDLC ultra-capacitor, battery-free design with a 10-year storage design life. Applied to a control power point that runs continuously on standby, it can operate long-term without periodic battery replacement.
